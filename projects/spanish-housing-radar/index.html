The problem

Spotting a genuinely under-priced flat means comparing it against the right peers — same neighbourhood, similar size — not a city-wide average. I wanted a pipeline that ingests listings continuously and scores each one against local benchmarks, so a good deal is quantified, not a gut feeling.

The hard part isn't the z-score, it's the honesty. Spanish listings are sparse at neighbourhood level, and a flat compared only against its own barrio is often compared against itself — a z-score of 0 and a meaningless “fair” verdict. The engineering is deciding which benchmark a listing has earned, and telling the user which one it got.

Key decisions & why

  • A barrio is trusted as far as its data earns. The score used to switch at a fixed cut-off — 8 listings and the barrio median counted in full, 7 and not at all. Measured, that was wrong: listings vary inside a barrio far more than barrios differ inside a district, so a barrio of 8 deserves about 57% weight. The benchmark now starts from the district and the barrio pulls it by an empirical-Bayes weight, n / (n + k), with k estimated in SQL on every build. For rents the data showed no barrio effect at all, so rents are scored against their district — and the app says so.
  • Implausible and stale listings set aside, in the open. A one-bedroom rent listed at 707 m² — a typo for 70 — was once the top “great deal”, and it widened its barrio's spread for every other rent there. Listings under a quarter of, or over four times, their city's median €/m² are now kept out of every benchmark, as are listings unseen for two months; the app lists what was removed and why.
  • Neutral, not extreme, when dispersion is zero. A benchmark built from a single comparable has a standard deviation of 0 or NULL, which would divide to infinity and snap the score to ±3. Coalescing that case to 0 keeps a thin benchmark from manufacturing a fake bargain — a bug that would have looked exactly like a great find.
  • Search cards over detail pages. A search page costs a flat 25 Scrapfly credits and returns ~30 listings; a detail page costs 25–29 and returns one. For a benchmark engine, breadth of comparables beats per-listing depth — the trade-off is no per-listing coordinates.
 3_gold/fct_listings_scored.sql
-- how far to trust a barrio: within- over between-barrio variance
case when n_barrios >= {{ min_barrios }} and between_var > 0
     then within_var / between_var end as shrinkage_k,
-- the barrio pulls its district's median by n / (n + k)
barrio_weight * barrio_median_ppsqm
    + (1 - barrio_weight) * parent_median_ppsqm as benchmark_median_ppsqm,
-- ...then a clamped z-score, neutral when dispersion is zero
round(greatest(-3.0, least(3.0,
    coalesce((price_per_sqm - benchmark_median_ppsqm)
             / nullif(benchmark_stddev_ppsqm, 0), 0)
)), 3) as ppsqm_z_score

Honest status

Scraping runs every Monday for València only, sale and rent, inside Scrapfly's free monthly credits — depth in one city rather than breadth. A one-off snapshot of seven other cities is still in the warehouse history but has aged out of scoring. The bugs worth knowing about were caught by the pipeline's own checks, not by a user: a data-currency test found the INE price index frozen on a table INE had rebased, a regression test found the buy-vs-rent comparison charging closing costs twice, and a rebuild into the CI schema showed a full refresh would have wiped the snapshot history — now guarded. What I'd do differently: measure the benchmark's trust in a barrio from day one instead of picking a cut-off.
